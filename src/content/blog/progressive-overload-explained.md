If you do the same workout with the same weights for months, your body has no reason to change. Progressive overload simply means **asking your muscles to do a little more over time**. It's the single most important principle in strength training.

## Five ways to progress

You don't have to add weight every session. Any of these count:

1. **Add weight.** The classic: 2.5 kg on the bar once you hit your target reps.
2. **Add reps.** 3 sets of 8 becomes 3 sets of 9, then 10.
3. **Add sets.** Move from 3 to 4 working sets over a training block.
4. **Improve technique and control.** A slower lowering phase or a deeper squat with the same weight is harder.
5. **Shorten rest** (useful for fat loss and conditioning, less so for pure strength).

## The double progression method

The simplest system for beginners and intermediates:

- Pick a rep range, for example 8–12.
- Use a weight you can lift for 8 good reps.
- Each session, try to add one rep to some sets.
- When you hit 12 reps on every set, add a little weight and start again at 8.

Use the [one-rep max calculator](/tools/one-rep-max-calculator) to pick sensible starting weights.

## How fast should you progress?

Beginners can often add weight to big lifts every week or two. Intermediate lifters progress more slowly, over weeks or months. That's normal. Progress is measured across a training block, not every single session.

## When progress stalls

- **Check sleep, food and stress first.** Most plateaus are recovery problems.
- **Take a deload week.** Cut volume by about 40–50% for a week, then return.
- **Change the rep range** for a block, for example from 8–12 to 5–8.

This is periodization in action: planning training in phases so you keep improving. It's the idea PeriodicFit is built on.
